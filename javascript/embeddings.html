How Embeddings Work

Everything on this page runs on a small built-in corpus of a few hundred short, templated sentences about animals, foods, royalty and people, places, and verbs -- no downloads, no server, no libraries. The steps below turn that plain text into a point in space for every word, purely by counting which words tend to appear near each other.

1. Tokenize and build a vocabulary

Every sentence is lowercased and split into word tokens. A short list of stopwords ("the", "a", "is", "and", and so on) is dropped, along with any word that shows up fewer than 3 times in the whole corpus -- both are too common or too rare to say much about meaning.

Word Count

2. Count co-occurrence within a window

For each pair of vocabulary words, count how often one appears within words of the other, in the same sentence. Words that show up in the same kinds of sentences build up a high count together. Here's that count for a handful of words from different clusters -- the full matrix covers every vocabulary word by every other one.

3. Convert counts to PPMI

Raw counts favor common words: "the" co-occurs with everything simply because it's everywhere. Positive pointwise mutual information (PPMI) corrects for that by comparing the actual co-occurrence rate to what chance alone would predict, given how common each word is on its own -- then clips anything below chance to zero, since "these two words avoid each other" isn't a useful signal here. 4. Reduce to dense vectors with truncated SVD

The PPMI matrix has one row per vocabulary word, so it's already a (very high-dimensional, mostly-zero) vector space. Truncated SVD compresses each word down to a short, dense vector -- 10 numbers instead of one per vocabulary word -- that keeps the directions of strongest variation and discards the rest. This page finds those directions with power iteration: repeatedly multiplying a vector by the matrix pulls it toward the dominant direction, and deflation removes that direction before repeating the process for the next one. The starting vector is a fixed formula rather than random, so the same corpus always produces the same vectors.

5. Project to two dimensions and explore

The first two of those ten numbers -- the two strongest directions of variation -- are enough to plot every word on a flat scatter plot. This is the real projection, plotted exactly where the math puts it: nothing here is nudged or spread out for legibility, so two points landing close together (or, occasionally, right on top of each other) means the pipeline actually found those two words hard to tell apart from context alone.

    Analogies

    The classic embedding party trick: take word a, subtract word b, add word c, and see which word's vector lands closest to the result. "king - man + woman" is the standard example. This corpus gives "king" and "queen" a shared "royal" context (crown, throne, council) that "man" and "woman" don't have, and gives "man"/"king" a shared "he" context while "woman"/"queen" share a "she" context -- the two ingredients an analogy like this needs.       How it works

      Every step above -- co-occurrence counting, PPMI, truncated SVD -- fits under "count how often words appear near each other, then compress that into a short vector." That family is sometimes called a count-based embedding, and it's how some of the earliest word vector methods worked.

      word2vec, the method usually associated with "king - man + woman = queen," takes a different route to a similar place: instead of counting co-occurrences directly, it trains a small neural network to predict a word from its neighbors (or its neighbors from the word), and the network's internal representation of each word becomes its vector. It never builds a co-occurrence matrix at all, but it ends up learning similar structure, because predicting context well requires noticing which words share contexts -- the same underlying signal this page counts directly.

      Both of those give each word exactly one vector, no matter the sentence. Contextual models like BERT don't: they compute a different vector for a word in each sentence it appears in, built from that sentence's other words. That's what lets a contextual model tell "bank" (of a river) apart from "bank" (that holds money) -- something a single fixed vector per word, like the ones on this page, cannot do.
