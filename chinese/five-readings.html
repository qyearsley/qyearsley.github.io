Chinese characters spread across East and Southeast Asia long before Mandarin became standard. Japanese, Korean, and Vietnamese each borrowed a layer of Chinese vocabulary wholesale, at a time closer to Middle Chinese than to modern Mandarin -- and Cantonese branched off from a common ancestor early enough that it kept some things Mandarin later dropped. Reading the same character's five reflexes side by side shows some of what Mandarin lost on its way to becoming Mandarin: the checked-tone endings -p, -t, and -k, and the distinction between nasal endings -m and -n.

Romanization: Hanyu Pinyin for Mandarin, Jyutping for Cantonese, Hepburn for Japanese on'yomi, Revised Romanization (with Hangul) for Korean, and Quốc Ngữ for Vietnamese.

Unverified readings. These readings have not been checked against Unihan or another dictionary yet. They are here to show the sound patterns, not to serve as a reference.

What Mandarin lost

Middle Chinese syllables could end in a stop consonant -- -p, -t, or -k -- forming the "checked tone" (入聲 rùshēng) category. Mandarin simply dropped these final stops, folding each syllable into one of its four modern tones by other rules. Middle Chinese also had three nasal endings, -m, -n, and -ng; Mandarin merged -m into -n, so words that used to end differently now rhyme. Cantonese, the Sino-Korean vocabulary layer, and Sino-Vietnamese vocabulary all kept these distinctions much better than Mandarin did; Japanese on'yomi kept the checked endings (approximated, since Japanese doesn't allow a syllable to end in a bare consonant) but merged -m into -n on its own, independently of Mandarin.
